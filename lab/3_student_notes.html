Understanding KNN — In Plain Words

The idea behind K-Nearest Neighbours, explained the simple way — using the same patient example from lab.

The story so far

You work in a small clinic. A new patient walks in with a tumour that needs checking. The clinic already has records of 569 old patients — for each one, a doctor already decided: malignant (dangerous) or benign (safe).

The question is simple: can we use those 569 old records to help the new patient, quickly?

So, what is KNN?

KNN stands for K-Nearest Neighbours. The idea is honestly very simple:

"Find the old patients most similar to the new one, and go with what most of them were."

Think of it like this: you just moved into a new neighbourhood and want to know the best pizza place nearby. You ask your 5 nearest neighbours. If 4 out of 5 say the same place — you'd probably try that one too. You didn't need a formula. You just asked the people closest to you.

That's it. No complicated equation is built ahead of time. KNN simply looks at "who is closest?" every single time a new patient arrives.

Why do we "scale" the numbers first?

Imagine comparing two patients using just two numbers: height in centimetres (say, 150–190) and a small measurement like cell smoothness (say, 0.05–0.15).

If you just subtract the numbers directly, height differences (which can be 20, 30, 40) will always look "bigger" than smoothness differences (which are tiny, like 0.02) — even if smoothness actually matters just as much for diagnosis.

Scaling simply puts every measurement on the same footing, so no single number unfairly dominates the comparison just because it happens to be a bigger number.

How many neighbours should we ask? (Choosing K)

K just means "how many nearest neighbours do we listen to?"

  • Too few (K = 1): you're trusting just one neighbour. If that one happens to be a bit unusual, you get a bad answer.
  • Too many (K = 50): you start listening to people who aren't really "neighbours" anymore — their opinions drown out the ones who actually matter.
  • Somewhere in the middle usually works best — enough opinions to be reliable, but not so many that it gets noisy.

This is a simple sketch of the idea, not exact numbers from the lab — the real shape depends on your actual data.

When does KNN struggle?

  • Too many old patients: KNN compares the new patient to everyone, every single time. With millions of records, that gets slow.
  • Too many measurements: when you're comparing across hundreds of numbers at once, "closeness" starts to lose its meaning — everyone starts looking equally far from everyone else.
  • Forgetting to scale: as we saw above, one large-numbered measurement can quietly take over the whole comparison.

Quick recap

  • KNN looks at the K closest old cases and goes with the majority answer.
  • No formula is built in advance — it compares fresh, every time.
  • Scale your numbers first, so every measurement gets a fair say.
  • Choosing K is a balance — not too small, not too large.
  • KNN isn't great for huge datasets or huge numbers of measurements.
