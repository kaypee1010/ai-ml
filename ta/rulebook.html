The whole thing in ten lines

If you read nothing else this week, read this. Everything below is the detail behind it.

  1. Teams are four people. Two teams of five already exist and no more are allowed.
  2. Your topic is locked to your team. One topic, one team, no swaps, no exceptions.
  3. In Week 4 you build the stupidest thing that works. That is your baseline, and every later claim is measured against it.
  4. Never report a number without saying what you compared it to.
  5. Anything with randomness in it runs ten times. Report the spread, not your best run.
  6. Write every run into experiments.csv the day you run it, not in Week 13.
  7. AI tools are allowed for learning, boilerplate and debugging. Code you cannot explain is not.
  8. Anything involving people needs written consent and ethics clearance before you collect a single response.
  9. In Week 12 you stop building. Weeks 13 and 14 are for writing the paper.
  10. At the viva you are questioned alone, about your own work. "I did the data part" is not an answer.

SECTION 1

Teams, and what the fifth member owns

Week 1

Four students is the standard team. Five is allowed but limited, and it is not a lighter share of the same work.

Size

238 students do not divide evenly into teams of four: 59 teams of four leaves two students with nobody to join. The cohort therefore runs exactly two five-member teams, which makes 2 × 5 plus 57 × 4 = 238, and nobody is stranded.

RuleDetail
4Standard team size. No approval needed.
5Allowed, but capped at 2 such teams across the whole cohort, first come first served. One already exists, so exactly one place remains. Once it is taken the registration page accepts fours only.
3Not permitted. If your team falls to three because someone withdraws, tell me immediately — I will merge or reassign, and you will not be penalised for it.
6+Not permitted under any circumstances.

Members may come from any section. Teams cannot be changed after Week 2.

What the fifth member owns

A fifth person with no defined territory becomes a passenger by Week 6, and the viva will expose it in front of everyone. So the fifth slot has a named job, and it is the job most teams skip:

  • The baseline. Implement and own the simple comparison method your module brief names. Every claim your team makes is measured against this.
  • The spread. Run every stochastic result across ten seeds and produce the ranges. Nobody else on the team reports a bare number again.
  • The failure analysis. Find where the system breaks, characterise it, and explain why. This becomes the discussion section of the paper.
  • The experiment log. Own experiments.csv and keep it current from Week 4, not reconstructed in Week 13.
Why this is a real job, not a consolation prize These four things account for most of the marks in Experimental results and most of what earns credit in Discussion. They also cannot be faked at the viva, because the questions are simply "what was your baseline, and how far ahead of it did you finish?" and "show me your variance across seeds."

Three rules that follow from this

5·1

Every paper carries a contribution table

One row per member, naming which of the seven marked components that person owned. It sits immediately before the references. All members sign it before submission — a typed name is fine, but every member must have read it.

This is not bureaucracy. It is the document I hold you to at the viva, and it is your protection if a teammate disappears in Week 10.

5·2

Five-member teams must deliver the required extension

Your module brief names one extension. For a team of four it is expected but assessed with some latitude. For a team of five it is a hard deliverable: no extension, no marks in Experimental results beyond the baseline comparison.

The extension is where the interesting work is. Five people is enough to reach it.

5·3

Viva time scales with the team

Ten minutes of demo plus five minutes of individual questioning per member. A four-member team occupies 30 minutes; a five-member team occupies 35. Plan your Week 15 slot accordingly — the schedule will be published in Week 12 and will not flex on the day.

Marks do not change with team size Both sizes are marked out of 40 on the same rubric. A fifth member means the work goes deeper, not that each person does less. If marks scaled with size, every team would suddenly be five people.
SECTION 2

Six ground rules

Week 1 · live with it

These are referenced by number in feedback and at the mid-term review. "You have a Rule 4 problem" should mean something to you.

1

Follow your track's data contract

Your track charter defines the files, column names and units your track shares. Read them, write them, and never change them unilaterally — another team's code looks up your columns by name.

  • Never rename a column. Add new ones at the end only.
  • Dates YYYY-MM-DD, times HH:MM in 24-hour form.
  • State units once and keep them: km, litres, rupees.
  • A missing value is an empty cell. Never NA, never -1, never 0 — zero means zero.
  • Keep ID prefixes and padding: BK00042, not 42.

Some modules stand alone and have no shared contract — your track charter says which. Rules 2 to 6 still apply to you in full.

2

Your output is a product, not a by-product

Whatever your module produces goes in results/ with a README beside it stating three things: what each column means with its units, the exact command that regenerates it, and what must be true for the numbers to hold.

If another team cannot use your output without messaging you, it is not finished.

3

Every repository looks the same

<track>-<module>-<short-name>/
├── README.md
├── requirements.txt
├── .gitignore
├── experiments.csv
├── data/
│   ├── raw/          (never edit anything in here)
│   └── processed/
├── src/              (data_loader.py, model.py, train.py, evaluate.py)
├── notebooks/
├── results/
└── paper/

Lowercase, hyphens, no spaces. Commit messages describe what changed: add water usage regression, R2 = 0.71. "fix", "final", "update" and "asdf" are not commit messages, and I read the history.

4

Set your seed, report the spread

import random, numpy as np
SEED = 42
random.seed(SEED)
np.random.seed(SEED)

Anything involving randomness — a genetic algorithm, a neural network, a train/test split — runs at least ten times with different seeds, and you report the average and the spread.

  • Wrong: Our GA found a route of 84.2 km.
  • Right: Our GA averaged 86.1 km over 10 seeds; best 84.2, worst 89.7, standard deviation 1.8.

One run is not a result. It is an anecdote.

5

Every number needs a comparison and a range

Two questions cost more marks than anything else in this project. Ask them of every number you write down.

Compared to what? 87% means nothing alone. Compared to guessing the most common answer? Compared to what a person currently does by hand? Name your baseline before you name your result.

How sure are you? Two models at 85% and 87% are often the same model wearing different hats. Report a range, and never claim an improvement you cannot defend.

A team reporting 71% with a proper baseline and honest error bars scores above a team reporting 94% with neither. That is not a technicality — it is the main thing this module is teaching you.

6

Keep an experiment log

One file, experiments.csv, in the repository root. One row per run, written the moment you run it.

run_id, date, who, what_changed, main_metric, value, seed, notes
R014, 2026-09-12, Priya, added weather features, MAE, 4.21, 42, better on weekends

In Week 13 you will be writing the paper and trying to remember why run 14 beat run 11. This file is the answer. Teams that skip it lose about two days.

SECTION 3

The 15 weeks

Every week

Every team in every track follows this rhythm, applied to its own problem. That is deliberate: it means your mentor can help any team in any week, and it means you always know what "behind" looks like.

WEEK 1Set up
  • Read the rulebook, your track charter and your module page together, as a team
  • Create the repository using the structure in Rule 3
  • Everyone installs Python, clones the repo, and pushes one commit
  • Split the work: data, model, evaluation, writing — and for teams of five, assign the baseline-and-spread role
DONE WHENThe repository exists, there is one commit from every member, and roles are written in the README.
WEEK 2Understand the data

Build nothing this week. Just look. Load your data, print shapes and types, count missing values in every column, plot every distribution, and write down what surprises you.

DONE WHENnotebooks/01_exploration.ipynb exists and five surprises are recorded in the README.

Teams rush this week and pay for it in Week 9, when a data problem they never noticed breaks the model.

WEEK 3Read what others have done
  • Find 8–10 papers on your problem: Google Scholar, arXiv, Papers with Code
  • For each, three lines: what they did, what data, what you can borrow
  • Find two public datasets related to your problem as backup
  • Decide which methods you will actually try, and why
DONE WHENA literature table with 8–10 rows exists. This becomes Section 2 of your paper almost unchanged.
WEEK 4Build the stupidest thing that works

The most important week, and the one teams most want to skip. Build the simplest possible solution — not a good one, a working one. Then measure it properly.

DONE WHENA baseline runs end to end and its score is in experiments.csv.

This number is your opponent for eight weeks. Without it you cannot prove anything you build later is any good.

WEEK 5Publish your output format

The integration handshake, for tracks whose modules feed one another. Publish what your module will produce before building it: exact columns, a dummy file with five fake rows, confirmed with the teams who depend on you. Your track charter names those teams; if it names none, see the note below.

DONE WHENA dummy output file is in your repo and the teams you depend on have confirmed theirs.

Teams that skip this week are the ones whose modules do not fit in Week 14. If your track charter does not name any team you exchange files with, your module stands alone — use this week to freeze your evaluation protocol instead: the exact metric, the exact split, and the exact baseline you will be judged against for the remaining ten weeks.

WEEK 6Build the real version

Implement the main method from your roadmap. Get it running end to end even if it performs badly, then compare it against the Week 4 baseline and log the result.

DONE WHENYour real method runs, and you know whether it beats the baseline yet.
WEEK 7Measure it honestly
  • Choose the right metric — accuracy is wrong on imbalanced data, RMSE is wrong when large errors are catastrophic
  • Run ten seeds, report average and spread
  • Split data properly: by date for time series, by group where groups exist
  • Look at your failure cases one by one
DONE WHENA results table shows baseline, your method, and error bars on both.
WEEK 8Mid-term review
  • Ten-minute demo: problem, baseline, method, numbers
  • Show one thing that is not working — every team has one, and hiding it wastes the review
  • Bring experiments.csv. You will be asked for it
DONE WHENDemo delivered, feedback written down, plan for Weeks 9–12 updated.
WEEK 9Improve, and test the joins

Two jobs. Improve what the mid-term exposed, and load another team's real output file and run your code against it.

DONE WHENYour code runs on a real file from another team. Bugs found now cost hours; found in Week 14 they cost the grade.
WEEK 10The required extension

Your module brief names one. This is the week for it, and it is what separates a working assignment from a project worth reading. For five-member teams this is a hard deliverable — see Rule 5·2.

DONE WHENThe extension is implemented and its results are logged.
WEEK 11Break your own work
  • What happens with half the data? A tenth?
  • What about messy inputs — missing values, wrong units, wild outliers?
  • Where does it fail, and can you explain why?
  • If it is slow, how slow, on what hardware?
DONE WHENYou have a written list of your module's limits. This becomes your discussion section, and it is where the real marks live.
WEEK 12Freeze
  • Stop building. Genuinely stop
  • Final run of everything, all seeds, all logged
  • Produce every figure and table the paper needs
  • Clean the repo: no dead code, no stray notebooks, complete requirements.txt
  • Someone outside the team clones it fresh and runs it. Fix whatever breaks
DONE WHENResults are frozen, the repo is clean, and a stranger can run it.
WEEK 13Write the first half

One section per day. Mon: abstract and introduction. Tue: literature review, mostly done in Week 3. Wed: methodology. Thu: experimental setup. Fri: rewrite Monday's abstract, which will be wrong by now.

DONE WHENSections 1–5 are drafted.
WEEK 14Write the second half
  • Results, discussion, conclusion, references
  • Every figure gets a caption, axis labels, and a mention in the text
  • Build slides: 10–12, one point each, 18pt or larger
  • Write INTEGRATION.md and the contribution table
  • Run Turnitin, target under 20%
DONE WHENFull paper drafted, slides built, integration note and contribution table written.
WEEK 15Present
  • Rehearse the demo three times. Time it
  • Record a backup video in case the live demo fails
  • Each member prepares to answer: what did you build, and how does it work?
  • Submit everything
DONE WHENSubmitted, presented, and every member has answered for their own work.
Viva reality check Each of you is questioned individually. "I did the data part" is not an answer. Know your own code line by line, and roughly what everyone else's does.

How you are marked

Week 8

40 marks, converted to the 30-mark UA scale as per assessment policy. Same rubric for teams of four and five.

ComponentMarksWhat actually earns them

Methodology and implementation10Code runs, is organised, and anyone can reproduce your results

GitHub repository5Structure followed, README clear, commit history real
Technical paper5IEEE format, 6–8 pages, complete, contribution table present
Presentation and viva5Clear demo, and each member defends their own contribution

Reliably loses marks

  • A number with no baseline — "we got 89%" invites "compared to what?"
  • A single run of anything random
  • A repository that will not run on another machine
  • A member who cannot explain their own contribution

Reliably gains them

  • Honesty about what did not work, and why
  • A failure analysis with specific examples
  • An unexpected result, checked carefully and reported rather than buried
  • Numbers a reader could reproduce from your repo alone
Depth beats accuracy A 60% model with genuine insight scores above an 85% model with none. This is the single most reliable way to do well here, and every year some teams do not believe it until the marks come back.
SECTION 6

AI tools

Week 1

Permitted, expected even, with one condition that is absolute.

You can

  • Learn and brainstorm — "explain how convolutional layers work"
  • Generate boilerplate — "a Flask template with three routes"
  • Debug — "why does my model return NaN during training?"

You cannot

  • Submit code you cannot explain line by line
  • Have AI write your training script and submit it unchanged
  • Submit AI-written paper text without rewriting it in your own words
  • Let AI do your thinking — you must understand your own results
If you cannot explain it, do not submit it At the viva you will be asked to explain every algorithm and why you chose it, every major function, what your results mean, and what you personally did. Acknowledge any AI tool you used in the paper. Turnitin target is under 20% with proper citation.
SECTION 7

Research ethics

Week 2 · before collecting

Several topics involve human participants or institutional data. These rules are not negotiable and they cost you nothing if you plan for them in Week 2 rather than Week 12.

  • Human participants require written informed consent and institutional ethics clearance before any collection begins. Not after. Not retrospectively.
  • Participants may withdraw at any point, and their data goes with them.
  • Institutional data must be anonymised before modelling: hashed identifiers, contact and medical fields removed.
  • State dataset provenance plainly, including when data is simulated rather than observed.
  • Any system that touches people needs a written account of who is harmed when it is wrong.
No fabricated data. Ever. Inventing results, padding a dataset, or reporting a run that never happened ends the project and goes to the department. If your numbers are disappointing, report the disappointing numbers — that path leads to a good mark, and the other one leads nowhere.

SECTION 10

Questions teams ask

When stuck

Our accuracy is only 65%. Are we failing?

No. A 65% model with a proper baseline, honest error bars and real failure analysis scores above an 85% model with none.

Can our team have five members?

Only two teams in the entire cohort may have five members, and one of those places is already taken. The registration page shows whether the last place is still free. Read Section 1 before you decide — the fifth slot is a defined job, not a spare seat.

Someone in our team has stopped responding.

Tell me in the week it happens, not in Week 14. Each student is questioned individually at the viva and marked on their own contribution, so you are not carrying their marks — but you may be carrying their work, and that is fixable if I know early.

A team we depend on is behind.

Build against your Week 5 dummy file and keep going. Tell your mentor. Never let another team's delay stop your work.

Can we use a pre-trained model such as BERT or ResNet?

Yes, transfer learning is valid. Document what you changed and what you trained yourself.

Can we adapt a solution found on GitHub?

Study and adapt, never copy-paste. You must be able to modify every line, which means you must understand every line.

Do all of us need to write code?

Ideally yes, and roles can still differ. But everyone must understand the full pipeline, because everyone is asked about it.

Our results contradict the paper we based this on.

Excellent. Check carefully, and if it holds, that is a genuine finding. Write it up rather than bury it.

We finished early.

Do the extension properly. Then break your own work — less data, messier data, different assumptions. Then help a team that is behind.

Build one real part properly, prove it works with honest numbers, and make sure it fits with everyone else's.
